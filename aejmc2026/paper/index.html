Partisan Media Exposure and Broadcast Network News Credibility

The Roles of Uncritical Patriotism and Hostile Media Perceptions

View the poster Request the manuscript
Working paper
  • Authors Siman Liu, University at Albany Masahiro Yamamoto, University at Albany Jay Hmielowski, University of Florida
  • In One Sentence Two people, one broadcast, two “biased” verdicts: we find that national loyalty is the hidden lens bending how partisans judge the news.

Abstract

Public confidence in broadcast network news is split along partisan lines—even in outlets that work hard to appear neutral. Drawing on selective exposure and hostile media perception (HMP), this study asks a sharper question: when does partisan media exposure lead people to see broadcast news as biased, and in turn less credible? Using a party-relative approach that separates in-group from opposing-party media, and a matched YouGov survey (N = 735), we find that exposure does not push everyone the same way. Its effect depends on uncritical patriotism—an unquestioning loyalty to the nation. Among high-loyalty audiences, both in-group and opposing-party exposure were more strongly linked to hostile perceptions, and through them, to lower perceived credibility of ABC, CBS, and NBC.

  • Media trust
  • Hostile media perception
  • Partisan media exposure

Core Findings

Partisan media exposure isn't a single, uniform force—the same media diet lands differently depending on who's watching. What separates them is loyalty: uncritical patriotism acts as an evaluative lens, and where it runs high, both aligned and opposing-party exposure map more strongly onto seeing broadcast news as hostile. That hostility, in turn, tracks with lower credibility for mainstream networks that present themselves as balanced. In short, whether news feels fair may be less about the news itself than about the loyalties viewers bring to it.
